# The Shipwreck Computer That Was 2,000 Years Too Early

In the spring of 1900, a storm blew a Greek sponge-diving boat off course. They sheltered behind a rocky speck of an island called Antikythera, halfway between Crete and the mainland, and waited for the weather to break. While they waited, captain Dimitrios Kondos sent a diver named Elias Stadiatis down the cliff face in his canvas suit and copper helmet to see if there was anything interesting on the seabed at 45 meters.

Stadiatis came back up almost immediately, raving about a heap of rotting corpses and horses on the bottom. Kondos went down himself. The horses were bronze. The corpses were marble. Stadiatis had stumbled onto the largest ancient shipwreck ever found — a Roman-era cargo ship, roughly 70 BC, loaded with looted Greek statuary on its way to a buyer in Italy when it had sunk.

Over the next year, the Greek navy hauled up bronzes, glassware, amphorae, jewelry, the famous Antikythera Youth. National hero stuff. And in May 1902, an archaeologist named Valerios Stais was sorting through the lesser pieces in Athens when a lump of corroded bronze split open. Inside were gear teeth.

Not coarse mill-gears. Fine ones. Dozens of them, interlocked, with markings in ancient Greek.

The official position for the next half-century was that this could not possibly be what it looked like. Greeks did not build clockwork. Toothed bronze gear trains belong to medieval Europe, somewhere around the 14th century, when monks started building astronomical clocks for cathedrals. The Antikythera lump was 1,400 years older than that. So it had to be a fluke — a navigation tool, an astrolabe, maybe contamination from a later wreck. A few people published cautious papers. Most just shrugged.

## The man who refused to shrug

Derek J. de Solla Price was a British physicist who had drifted into the history of science. In 1951 he was visiting the National Museum in Athens, saw the lump, and could not let it go. For the next twenty-three years he would not shut up about it.

In 1971, working with the Greek nuclear physicist Charalampos Karakalos, Price did something nobody had thought to do: he X-rayed the thing. Or rather, gamma-rayed it, because the bronze was too dense for standard X-ray. The plates came back showing the interior — ghostly outlines of toothed wheels nested inside other toothed wheels, dials, pointers, an assembly that Price counted as having at least 30 gears in a box about the size of a shoebox.

He published in 1974, in *Transactions of the American Philosophical Society*, under the title *Gears from the Greeks*. His conclusion: the Antikythera mechanism was a mechanical computer for the heavens. Hand-cranked, geared, capable of showing the position of the sun and moon, the phases of the moon, and probably more. He worked out one differential gear arrangement that he believed calculated the difference between the lunar month and the solar year — to track the irregular Greek calendar.

Price died in 1983 still being treated, by parts of the field, as a slightly embarrassing enthusiast.

## The 2005 reckoning

Then in 2005, an international team led by Mike Edmunds of Cardiff University and the filmmaker Tony Freeth turned up at the museum with eight tons of equipment. They had built a custom microfocus CT scanner, on rollers, capable of imaging through bronze in three dimensions at a resolution Price could not have dreamed of. They also brought a Hewlett-Packard system called PTM Dome, which lights an object from dozens of angles and lets you read surface text that human eyes can no longer see.

What came out of those scans rewrote the field.

The mechanism had at least 37 gears, not 30. The teeth were cut at a precise angle of 60 degrees. The largest gear had 223 teeth — and 223 is the *Saros number*, the count of lunar months in a Saros cycle, the 18-year, 11-day, 8-hour interval after which solar and lunar eclipses repeat. Whoever built this knew the Saros and built a gear specifically to count it.

There were front and back faces. The front had a zodiac dial and an Egyptian calendar dial, with pointers showing the sun, the moon, and — almost certainly, from the lost gear count — the five planets known to antiquity: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn. There was a rotating silver-and-black ball on the front that displayed the lunar phase, driven by a contrarotating gear pair. (We invented that idea again in 1670.)

The back had two dials. The upper one was a spiral with 235 cells: the *Metonic cycle*, 19 years in which 235 lunar months equal 19 solar years almost exactly. The lower spiral had 223 cells: the Saros cycle, with little glyphs marking which months would contain eclipses, and even letters indicating the time of day the eclipse would occur. There was a small subsidiary dial, the *Olympiad dial*, that ticked over once every four years, naming which Panhellenic Games were upcoming — Olympia, Delphi, Nemea, Isthmia.

The text inscribed on the surface — a user manual, essentially — runs to roughly 3,500 Greek characters that have been recovered. It uses dialect features and color-of-eclipse vocabulary that match astronomical writing from a particular school of Greek astronomy. There is a strong argument, advanced in 2008 and again in 2014, that the device was made on Rhodes, possibly in the workshop of Hipparchus or his successors, around 150 to 100 BC. Cicero, writing a few decades later, casually mentions a device built by Archimedes that displayed the heavens, and another in the possession of his teacher Posidonius on Rhodes that did much the same. Most historians had taken these passages as Roman exaggeration. The shipwreck says: he meant it literally.

## The gap

This is the part that is hard to absorb.

After the Antikythera mechanism sank around 65 BC, nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known to have existed for at least 1,400 years. The Romans did not copy it. The Byzantines did not preserve it. The Islamic world built astrolabes and water clocks and lovely planetary dials, but nothing with this density of gearing. Geared clockwork as a continuous tradition restarts in Europe in the late 1200s.

Imagine an iPhone in a Pharaoh's tomb. Then imagine that the next phone shows up in 3400 AD. That is roughly the shape of the gap.

There are two ways to interpret this. One: the mechanism is the surviving tip of a much larger Hellenistic technical culture that simply did not transmit its knowledge. Hellenistic engineering produced steam toys (Hero of Alexandria), automated theaters, water-organs, and odometers. Most of it died with the libraries. We may be looking at one survivor of a dozen lost design lineages.

Two, more uncomfortable: it was a one-off. A genius or two on a small island made one or two of these for rich patrons, the patrons died, the apprentices scattered, and the knowledge evaporated. Civilizations do not reliably keep their best inventions. They have to be written down, copied, taught, demanded by markets. Otherwise they sink, sometimes literally, with the boat.

## What it means to compute

The thing about the Antikythera mechanism that bothers me most is not the gears. It is what the gears mean.

To build a device that takes a hand crank as input and produces, as output, the position of Mars on a particular day in 87 BC — you have to first believe that the heavens are *legal*. That they obey rules. That the apparent madness of retrograde planetary motion can be encoded as a difference of two circular motions, and that those motions can be turned into the ratios of teeth on bronze wheels. You have to be willing to bet that the universe is a machine and that a small machine on a table can mirror it.

That is a philosophical bet, and the Greeks made it 1,800 years before Newton. The shipwreck near Antikythera is the surviving physical evidence that someone was not just speculating but actually building.

The next time you fish a phone out of your pocket and ask it where Saturn is tonight, remember: a Greek did that, with bronze and a hand-crank, and then the answer fell into the sea for 2,000 years. The question is how many other answers we have already lost, and how much of what we know now is one bad shipwreck away from being unknown again.
