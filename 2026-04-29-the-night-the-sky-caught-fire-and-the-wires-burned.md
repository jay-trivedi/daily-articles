# The Night the Sky Caught Fire and the Wires Burned

On the morning of September 1, 1859, an English amateur astronomer named Richard Carrington was doing what he did most clear mornings — projecting an image of the sun through his telescope onto a screen, carefully sketching the sunspots crawling across its face. He was thirty-three years old, the son of a brewer, wealthy enough to skip the family business and build his own observatory in Surrey. He wanted to be the man who finally explained sunspots.

What he saw at 11:18 a.m. instead made him the man who explained something far stranger.

Two patches on the sun, near a cluster of dark spots, suddenly flared up white. Not bright like the rest of the sun — *brighter*. Brighter than the photosphere itself. Carrington was so startled he ran out of his observatory to find someone else to confirm what he was seeing. By the time he came back, less than a minute later, the patches had already begun to fade. Within five minutes they were gone.

He sketched it anyway. So did Richard Hodgson, another English astronomer working independently nearby, who happened to be looking at the sun at the same instant. Two careful observers, two sketches, one short paper to the Royal Astronomical Society. They had just seen the first solar flare ever recorded by human eyes.

They had no idea what they had set in motion.

## Seventeen hours later

A solar flare is a release of energy. The big ones eject billions of tons of charged plasma off the sun's surface — what we now call a *coronal mass ejection*, or CME. That plasma takes its time crossing the ninety-three million miles to Earth. A typical CME takes three or four days. A fast one, two.

The Carrington CME took about seventeen and a half hours.

This is so fast it implies something extraordinary: a previous, smaller eruption a few days earlier had cleared the path, plowing the solar wind out of the way like a snowplow. The September 1 ejection then rode the empty corridor at unbelievable speed — somewhere around 5 to 10 million kilometers per hour — and slammed into Earth's magnetic field on the night of September 1–2.

What happened next is, to this day, the most extreme geomagnetic storm in recorded history.

## Auroras over Havana

Auroras are normally a polar phenomenon. You see them in Norway, Alaska, northern Canada. They happen because charged particles from the sun get funneled by Earth's magnetic field down into the upper atmosphere near the poles, where they collide with oxygen and nitrogen and make them glow.

When a storm is strong enough, the auroral oval pushes south. A bad storm puts the lights over Edinburgh. A really bad storm puts them over New York.

The Carrington event put them over **Havana**. Over Honolulu. Over Panama. Over Colombia, almost on the equator. Sailors in the Caribbean wrote in their logs that the entire sky was on fire — bright enough to read a newspaper by, at midnight. In the Rocky Mountains, gold miners woke up at one in the morning, saw the sky glowing red and green, assumed dawn had come, and started cooking breakfast. In Boston, the *New York Times* later reported that you could read fine print outdoors at 1 a.m. by aurora light alone.

This was beautiful. What happened to the telegraph wires was not.

## The wires that wrote by themselves

In 1859, the telegraph was the internet. There were about 200,000 kilometers of telegraph wire strung across North America and Europe, carrying news, market prices, and personal messages between cities at a speed that had felt, just twenty years earlier, like sorcery. The first transatlantic cable had been laid the previous year (and had already broken; a working one wouldn't come until 1866). The telegraph network was the marvel of the age, and it was absolutely, fatally exposed to what the sun had just done.

When a strong geomagnetic storm hits Earth, the rapid changes in the magnetic field induce electric currents in any long conductor that happens to be lying around. Long conductors like, say, hundreds of thousands of kilometers of telegraph wire.

What followed was something out of science fiction. Telegraph operators across both continents found their equipment going haywire. Some got knocked off their stools by electric shocks. Some watched as sparks leapt from the keys to their fingers. Paper tape caught fire spontaneously inside telegraph machines. In Pittsburgh, an operator reported flames coming out of his switchboard.

Then came the part that made everyone realize this was not an ordinary storm.

In Boston, an operator named George Walker disconnected his battery — pulled the entire power source out of the system — and tried to send a message to Portland, Maine, anyway. It went through perfectly. The wires were so saturated with auroral current that the telegraph was running on pure sunlight. Two operators between Boston and Portland kept up an entire conversation for two hours that way, batteries removed, powered by the storm itself.

Eventually, someone wrote down what they had said. The first message Boston sent to Portland under solar power was a polite suggestion: that they should both stop working for a while and let the aurora pass.

## The next time

Earth gets hit by minor geomagnetic storms all the time. A few times a decade we get a serious one. In March 1989, a storm a fraction of the Carrington event's strength took out the entire Quebec power grid in 90 seconds, leaving six million people without electricity for nine hours. Transformers melted. Backup systems failed. Trading halted at the Toronto Stock Exchange because the disk drives went down.

In July 2012, a Carrington-class CME erupted off the side of the sun and tore through Earth's orbit at the kind of speed Carrington's storm had — except Earth had been at that exact spot in space nine days earlier. We missed it by about a week. Pete Riley, a physicist at Predictive Science, calculated in 2012 that the probability of a Carrington-level storm hitting Earth in any given decade is roughly **12 percent**. Not 0.12. *Twelve.*

If it hits today, the telegraph wires won't catch fire — there aren't any. But everything else will.

The modern equivalents of those long conductors are continent-spanning power transmission lines, and the things plugged into them are extra-high-voltage transformers — vast machines the size of a small house, custom-built, with delivery times measured in years. A 2008 report by the U.S. National Academy of Sciences estimated that a modern Carrington event would knock out hundreds of these transformers across North America. Replacing them would take, optimistically, four to ten years. The economic damage would run between one and two trillion dollars in the first year alone — five to ten times the cost of Hurricane Katrina, and that's just the United States.

GPS would fail. Satellite communications would go dark, at least temporarily, as electronics fried in orbit. International banking, which runs on satellite-synchronized time signals accurate to nanoseconds, would seize up. Long-haul aviation, which depends on satellite navigation over the poles, would reroute or stop. Water treatment, which depends on electric pumps. Refrigeration. Hospitals on emergency generators that were never designed to run for years.

We have warning systems now — satellites stationed a million miles upstream of Earth that can give us, in the best case, about 15 to 60 minutes of advance notice once the CME passes them. That is enough time to shut down a power grid in a controlled way, parking the transformers safely. It is not enough time to argue about whether to do it. The decision would have to be automatic, or near-automatic, and the consequences of being wrong — pulling the plug on a continent for a storm that turns out to be smaller than predicted — are large enough that no one is in a hurry to commit to the protocol.

## What Carrington didn't know

When Richard Carrington wrote up his observation for the Royal Astronomical Society later that month, he was careful. He noted the brilliant flash. He noted that, the following night, "a magnetic storm of unusual violence" had been observed by the magnetometers at Kew Observatory. He even floated, gingerly, the possibility of a connection.

But he also added a sentence that has aged into one of science's great understatements: *"One swallow does not make a summer."*

He thought he might be jumping to conclusions. He was not. The link between solar flares and geomagnetic storms — the entire field of *space weather* — begins with that white flash on his sketch pad and the wires that burned that night.

We know now what he could not have known: that our star is not the steady lantern it appears to be from a backyard in Surrey. It is a churning, magnetic thing that occasionally throws billion-ton blobs of plasma at us at four million miles an hour, and it has done so many times in the past, and it will do so again. Tree-ring carbon-14 spikes show that storms several times stronger than Carrington's have hit Earth in 774 CE and 993 CE. Imagine those if they had landed on a civilization wired together by copper.

Imagine the next one landing on this one.

The interesting question is not whether Carrington's flash will repeat. It will. The interesting question is what we will be doing on the morning it arrives, what we are connected to, how much warning we accept, and which messages we will still be able to send — perhaps, if we are very lucky, on solar power, with the batteries pulled out, by aurora light.
