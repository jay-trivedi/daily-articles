# The Megacity That Lived by Water and Died of It

In the year 1296, a Chinese diplomat named Zhou Daguan sailed up the Mekong, walked into the Khmer capital of Angkor, and spent eleven months trying to make sense of what he was seeing. He was a careful observer — he wrote about the king's gold palanquin, the parasols of office, the women who bathed in rivers, the bronze tower at the city's heart. But the thing he could not stop describing was the water. Moats wider than a bowshot. Reservoirs that looked like inland seas. Canals so straight they seemed cut by a god's chisel. He was standing inside the largest preindustrial city humans had ever built, and almost everything about it — its food, its empire, its sheer existence — depended on moving water exactly where you wanted it, exactly when you wanted it.

A century and a half later, the city was empty. The canals had silted up. The reservoirs were dry or breached. The temples that had drawn pilgrims from Burma to Vietnam were swallowed by jungle so completely that when Henri Mouhot "rediscovered" Angkor Wat in 1860, he assumed Greeks or Romans must have built it, because surely no Asian people he knew of could have managed something so vast. He was, of course, completely wrong. The people who built it were the Khmer, and they had not vanished. They had simply lost the thing that made the megacity possible.

## A city built like a circuit board

The Khmer Empire began in 802 CE, when a king named Jayavarman II declared himself a *chakravartin* — a universal monarch — on a hilltop near the Tonle Sap. Over the next six hundred years, his successors did something almost no other premodern civilization managed: they engineered an entire landscape. Modern airborne LiDAR surveys, led most prominently by archaeologist Damian Evans starting around 2012, have peeled back the forest canopy and revealed what's actually underneath. The picture is staggering. Greater Angkor at its peak covered roughly 1,000 square kilometers — bigger than modern Paris, bigger than New York City. It had a population somewhere between 700,000 and one million people at a time when London had perhaps 50,000 and Paris maybe 200,000.

And it ran on water.

The Tonle Sap, the lake at the heart of Cambodia, has a strange habit. For half the year, the Mekong flows so hard that it actually pushes the Tonle Sap River *backwards*, swelling the lake to five times its dry-season size. The Khmer engineers caught this rhythm and bent it to their will. They built enormous rectangular reservoirs called *barays* — the West Baray alone is eight kilometers long, two kilometers wide, and held something like fifty million cubic meters of water. They threaded thousands of kilometers of canals through the floodplain. They graded the land so precisely that water moved by gravity from one zone to another, irrigating rice paddies that produced two and sometimes three harvests a year while the rest of the region managed one.

This is the part that doesn't quite click for modern readers. Angkor Wat — the stone temple everyone has seen on a postcard — is the *small* version of what the Khmer built. The temples are the visible ornament. The actual civilization was the hydraulic network. The temples sat inside it the way computer chips sit on a circuit board. Without the board, the chips do nothing.

## The system that became a trap

Suryavarman II finished Angkor Wat around 1150. Jayavarman VII, the empire's most ambitious king, built the colossal Bayon and the walled city of Angkor Thom in the decades after 1181. He also built 102 hospitals, 121 rest-houses along the empire's roads, and a road network reaching as far as modern Vientiane. Khmer power stretched from the Malay Peninsula into Yunnan. And then, slowly, things began to go wrong.

The trouble was that the hydraulic network had grown so large and so finely tuned that it had become brittle. Every channel assumed the upstream channel was working. Every baray assumed the canal feeding it was clear. The Khmer had cleared enormous tracts of forest in the watersheds north of Angkor to feed the city, and erosion was beginning to choke the inlets. There is direct archaeological evidence — work by Roland Fletcher's Greater Angkor Project — of canals being patched, rerouted, and abandoned because their gradients had slipped or their channels had filled with sand. By the 14th century, parts of the network were already failing in slow motion.

Then the climate hit.

## Two droughts and a flood

In 2010, a team led by Brendan Buckley at Columbia's Tree Ring Lab pulled cores from very old cypress trees — *Fokienia hodginsii* — growing in the Vietnamese highlands. The rings recorded monsoon strength going back more than seven centuries. What they showed was startling. From roughly 1345 to 1374, Southeast Asia suffered a megadrought of a severity not seen in modern records. The monsoon, which the entire Khmer system was tuned to, simply did not show up the way it was supposed to. Then, after a brief recovery, a second megadrought hit from about 1401 to 1425. And in between the droughts, there were savage monsoon years — when the rains finally came, they came too hard, and the silted, weakened canals couldn't take it. Sluice gates blew out. Embankments collapsed. The kind of failure that a healthy network repairs in a season became, in a starving city, permanent.

In 1431, after years of pressure from a rising Thai kingdom to the west, the army of Ayutthaya sacked Angkor. By then, the sack was almost a formality. The capital had already been bleeding people for decades — south, toward the Mekong delta, where rain-fed agriculture and river trade did not require a hydraulic miracle to survive. The Khmer court eventually settled near what is now Phnom Penh, where it has remained ever since.

The temples stayed. The forest came back. The water moved on.

## The lesson nobody likes

It's tempting to read Angkor as a fable about climate change, and the parallel is real — a society perfectly optimized for one rainfall regime cannot survive a different one. But the more uncomfortable lesson is about complexity. The Khmer didn't fail because they were primitive. They failed because they were *too sophisticated for their margin of error*. The same engineering that let them feed a million people in a swamp also meant that any single failure mode — a silted intake, a missed monsoon, a forest cleared one valley too far — could cascade through the entire network. Simpler neighbors with smaller cities and rain-fed farms had less to lose, and so they kept going while Angkor emptied out.

We tell ourselves that our cities, with their just-in-time supply chains and their exquisitely tuned power grids and their water systems built for the climate of fifty years ago, are different. Zhou Daguan, sitting in 1296 in what was then the most advanced city on Earth, would have told you the same thing about his hosts. He was writing in Angkor's golden afternoon, and he had no idea. The thing about hydraulic civilizations is that they look invincible right up until the water stops doing what you told it to.
